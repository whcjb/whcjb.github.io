# 希伯来书 4:12-13

<div class="owen-vg-sub">约翰·欧文注释 · Ver. 12, 13</div>

<div class="owen-scr" markdown="0"><p class="owen-scr-grk">Ζῶν γὰρ ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, καὶ ἐνεργὴς, καὶ τομώτερος ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον, καὶ διϊκνούμενος ἄχρι μερισμοῦ ψυχῆς τε καὶ πνεύματος, ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν, καὶ κριτικὸς ἐνθυμήσεων καὶ ἐννοιῶν καρδίας· καὶ οὐκ ἔστι κτίσις ἀφανὴς ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ· πάντα δὲ γυμνὰ καὶ τετραχηλισμένα τοῖς ὀφθαλμοῖς αὐτοῦ, πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος.</p><p class="owen-scr-en">For the word of God is living and powerful [or effectual], and sharper [more cutting, or cutting more] than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner [a discerning judge] of the thoughts and intents [conceptions] of the heart. Neither is there any creature that is not [apparently] manifest in his sight: but all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do, [or to whom we must give an account.]</p></div>

<details class="owen-philology" markdown="0"><summary>原文字词考据（希腊 / 希伯来 / 叙利亚 / 拉丁文逐词对照）</summary><p>Ζῶν γάρ, &quot;vivus enim.&quot; Syr. הִי חַיָּא, &quot;vivus est;&quot; it supplies הִי, &quot;est,&quot; as do all other translations, though there be an emphasis ofttimes in sundry languages in the omission of the verb substantive. Ours, &quot;quick,&quot; improperly; for that word doth more ordinarily signify &quot;speedy,&quot; than &quot;living:&quot; and I doubt not but many are deceived in this place through the ambiguity of that word.</p><p>Ὁ λόγος, &quot;sermo,&quot; &quot;verbum;&quot; so is that word promiscuously rendered by translators, though the first using of &quot;sermo&quot; in John 1. I caused some stir amongst them who had been long used to &quot;verbum.&quot; But these words are promiscuously used, both by the ancients and learned men of latter days. Ours, &quot;The word.&quot; Syr., מֶלְתֵתּ; the same word that it useth John 1:1, where the person of the Son of God is spoken of.</p><p>Καὶ ἐνεργής, &quot;et efficax;&quot; so all the Latin translators;—&quot;efficacious,&quot; &quot;effectual in operation,&quot; &quot;powerful:&quot; but that denotes the habit, this word intends the act,—&quot;effectually operative.&quot; Syr. סָעֲיָא וְכֻל, &quot;et omnino,&quot; or &quot;ad omnia efficax,&quot;—&quot;altogether efficacious;&quot; for ἐνεργὴς denotes a very intimate, active, powerful operation or efficacy. Rhem. &quot;forcible.&quot;</p><p>Καὶ τομώτερος, Vulg. Lat., Ari. Mon., &quot;penetrabilior.&quot; Scarce properly, for participles in &quot;bilis,&quot; are mostly passive; and in our language, &quot;penetrable&quot; is the description of a thing that may be pierced, or is easy so to be. Hence the Rhem. render it &quot;more piercing,&quot; properly. Beza, &quot;penetrantior,&quot; as Erasmus. Valla, and from him Erasmus, say they would render it &quot;incidentior,&quot; were that a proper Latin word. Ours, &quot;sharper;&quot; not so properly,—&quot;more cutting,&quot; or &quot;more piercing.&quot; Syr., טָכ וְתַרִיפָא, &quot;et longe penetrantior;&quot; &quot;and much more cutting,&quot; &quot;sharp,&quot; or &quot;piercing.&quot; It adds &quot;cal&quot; and &quot;tab,&quot; to express the form of the comparative degree used in the original.</p><p>Ὑπὲρ πᾱσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον, &quot;super omnem gladium ancipitem,&quot; &quot;above any two-edged sword.&quot; Ὑπέρ being added to the preceding comparative τομώτερος, eminently exalts one of the comparates above the other. Syr. פַוְמִיהָ דַּתְרֵין, &quot;before a sword with two mouths.&quot; Both the Hebrews and the Greeks call the edge of the sword its mouth,—στόμα τῆς μαχαίρας, &quot;the mouth of the sword,&quot;—it being that wherewith it devours. Beza, &quot;quovis gladio ancipiti.&quot; Eras., &quot;utrinque incidente.&quot; Arab., &quot;and in cutting sharper than a sword of two edges.&quot; Ethiopic, &quot;than a razor.&quot; Ours, &quot;than any two-edged sword.&quot;</p><p>Καὶ δι̇ι̇κνούμενος, &quot;et pertingens,&quot; &quot;et pertinget.&quot; Syr., וְעָאֲלָא, &quot;et ingreditur,&quot;—&quot;and entereth,&quot; &quot;reacheth unto,&quot; &quot;cometh into,&quot; &quot;pierceth into.&quot;</p><p>Ἄχρζ μερισμοῦ ψυχῆς τε καὶ πνεύματος, &quot;usque ad divisionem animæ et spiritus.&quot; Beza, &quot;animæ simul ac spiritus,&quot; &quot;both of soul and spirit;&quot; expressing the particle τε, which yet in some copies is wanting.</p><p>Ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν, &quot;compagumque et medullarum,&quot; &quot;of the joints and marrow.&quot; The Syriac adds וְגַרְמֵא, &quot;and of the bones.&quot; Ethiopic, et discernit animam ab anima, et quod noctescit a nocte; &quot;discerneth one soul from another, and that which is dark from night,&quot;—that is, the most secret things.</p><p>Καὶ κριτικός, &quot;et discretor.&quot; Vulg. Lat., &quot;et judicat,&quot; &quot;et dijudicat;&quot; &quot;judgeth,&quot; &quot;discerneth.&quot; &quot;Judex,&quot; &quot;criticus,&quot; &quot;and is a discerner;&quot; that is, one that discerneth by making a right judgment of things.</p><p>Ἐνθυμήσεων, &quot;cogitationum.&quot; Ethiopic, &quot;cogitationum desiderabilium,&quot; &quot;desirable thoughts;&quot; not without reason, as we shall see.</p><p>Καὶ ἐννοιῶν καρδίας. Vulg. Lat., Ari., Eras., &quot;intentionum cordis,&quot; &quot;of the intentions of the heart.&quot; Beza, &quot;conceptuum,&quot; &quot;conceptions.&quot; Ours, &quot;intents,&quot;—a word of a deeper sense. There may be &quot;conceptus&quot; where there is not &quot;intentio&quot; or &quot;propositum.&quot; Syr., &quot;the will of the heart.&quot; See Eph. 2:3.</p><p>Καὶ οὐκ ἔστι κτίσις, &quot;et non est creatura,&quot; &quot;and there is not a creature.&quot; Beza, &quot;et nulla est res creata,&quot; &quot;and there is no created thing;&quot; more proper in Latin, but a &quot;creature&quot; is common with us.</p><p>Ἀφανής. Beza, &quot;non manifesta.&quot; Ours, &quot;that is not manifest.&quot; Vulg. Lat., &quot;invisibilis.&quot; And the Rhem., &quot;invisible,&quot; not properly; &quot;not manifestly apparent.&quot; Syr., &quot;that is hid.&quot;</p><p>Πάντα δὲ γυμνά. Beza, &quot;imo omnia nuda,&quot; &quot;yea, all things are naked.&quot; Ours, &quot;but all things are naked.&quot;</p><p>Καὶ τετραχηλισμένα. Vulg. Lat., &quot;aperta,&quot; &quot;open.&quot; Beza, &quot;intime patentia,&quot; &quot;inwardly open.&quot; Erasm., &quot;resupinata,&quot; &quot;laid on their backs,&quot; &quot;open.&quot; Syr., וַגְלֵא, &quot;and manifest,&quot; or &quot;revealed.&quot; Πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος. Beza, &quot;quo cum nobis est negotium;&quot; which ours render, &quot;with whom we have to do.&quot; Vulg. Lat., &quot;ad quem nobis sermo,&quot; Rhem., &quot;to whom our speech is.&quot; Syr., &quot;to whom they give account.&quot; And the Arabic to the same purpose, &quot;before whom our trial or excuse must be,&quot; What help we may have in the understanding of the words from these various translations of them, we shall see in our consideration of the particulars of the text. The difficulty of the place hath caused me to inquire the more diligently into the sense of translators upon the words themselves.</p><p>Ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ. The whole exposition of these words depends on the subject spoken of, verse 12; that, therefore, we must diligently inquire into. This being rightly stated, the things spoken must be duly accommodated unto it; and in these two things doth the due exposition of these words consist. Now this subject is ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, &quot;the word of God.&quot; It is known that this name sometimes in the Scripture denotes the essential Word of God, sometimes the word spoken by him: or, λόγος Θεοῦ is either οὐσιωδής, that is, the eternal Son of God; or προφορικός, his enunciative word, the word of his will, his declared, written word. And the confounding of these is that which so entangleth the Quakers amongst us; or rather, is that whereby they endeavour to entangle others, and seduce &quot;unlearned and unstable souls.&quot; But all sorts of expositors are divided in judgment about which of these it is that is here intended. Amongst the ancients, Ambrose, with many others, contends that it is the essential and eternal Word of God which is spoken of. Chrysostom seems rather to incline to the written word. The expositors of the Roman church are here also divided. Lyra, Cajetan, Carthusianus, à Lapide, Ribera, with sundry others, pleaded for the essential Word. Gatenus, Adamus, Hessetius, Estius, for the word written. So do the Rhemists in their annotations, and particularly for the word of threatening. Amongst the Protestants, few judge the essential Word, or Son of God, Jesus Christ, to be intended. Jacobus Cappellus and Gomarus I have only met withal that are positively of that mind. Among the rest, some take it for the word of God preached in general, as Calvin; some for the threatenings of God, with the Rhemists; and some peculiarly for the gospel. Crellius waives all these, and contends that it is the decree of God which is designed; which when he comes to the explanation of, he makes it the same with his threatenings. I shall inquire with what diligence I can into the true and direct meaning of the Holy Ghost herein.</p></details>

<p class="owen-d0 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">First</b> I grant that the name here used, ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, &quot;the word of God,&quot; is ascribed sometimes to the essential Word of God, and sometimes to the enunciative word, or the Scripture, as inspired and written. That the Son of God is so called we shall show afterwards; and that the declaration of the will of God by the penmen of the Scripture is so termed, is obvious and acknowledged by all but only our Quakers. But testimonies are full, many, and pregnant to this purpose: Luke 5:1, &quot;The multitude pressed on him to hear τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ,&quot;—&quot;the word of God;&quot; where the word of God is directly distinguished from him that spake it, which was Jesus Christ. Chap. 8:11, &quot;The seed is ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ,&quot;—&quot;the word of God;&quot; that is, the word preached by Jesus Christ, the good sower of that seed, as the whole chapter declares. Chap. 11:28, &quot;Blessed are those that hear τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ, καὶ φυλάσσοντες αὐτόν,&quot;—&quot;the word of God, and keep it;&quot; that is, preserve it in their hearts, and obey it being heard. Mark 7:13, &quot;Making void τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ,&quot;—&quot;the word of God by your traditions.&quot; The word of God, that is, in his institutions and commands, is directly opposed to the traditions and commands of men, and so is of the same general nature. Acts 4:31, &quot;They were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and spake out τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ,&quot;—&quot;the word of God,&quot; the word which they preached, declaring Jesus Christ to be the Son of God. When Philip had preached the gospel at Samaria and many believed, it is said, Acts 8:14, that &quot;the apostles heard that Samaria had received τὸν λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ,&quot;—&quot;the word of God,&quot; or believed the doctrine of the gospel preached unto them. Chap. 12:24, Ὁ δὲ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ ηὔξανε καὶ ἐπληθύνετο,&quot;—&quot;But the word of God grew and multiplied;&quot; that is, upon the death of Herod it was more and more preached and received. 1 Cor. 14:36, &quot;Did the word of God go out from you, or came it to you alone?&quot; In like manner is it used in many other places. I have instanced in these to obviate the vain clamours of those men who will not allow the Scripture, or gospel as preached, to be called the word of God. So ὁ λόγος absolutely, &quot;the word,&quot; and &quot;the word of the gospel,&quot; &quot;the word preached,&quot; &quot;the word of Christ,&quot; are common notations of this declared word of God.</p>

<p class="owen-d0 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">Secondly</b> It is granted that the attributes and effects that are there ascribed unto the word of God may, in several senses, be applied to the one and the other of the things mentioned. That they are properly ascribed unto the eternal Son of God shall be afterwards declared. That in some sense also they may be applied unto the written word, other places of the Scripture, where things of the same nature are ascribed unto it, do manifest. Isa. 49:2; Ps. 45:5, 105:19, 107:20, 147:15, 18; Isa. 40:8, 55:11, are cited by Grotius to this purpose, whereof yet more do clearly confirm the assertion. For though the word of God be mentioned in them, yet in some of the places the essential Word of God, in most of them his providential word, the word of his power, is unquestionably intended. But see Hos. 6:5; 1 Cor. 14:24, 25.</p>

<p class="owen-d0 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">Thirdly</b> It must be acknowledged, that if the things here mentioned be ascribed unto the written word, yet they do not primarily and absolutely belong unto it upon its own account, but by virtue of its relation unto Jesus Christ, whose word it is, and by reason of the power and efficacy that is by him communicated unto it. And on the other hand, if it be the Son, or the eternal Word of God, that is here intended, it will be granted that the things here ascribed unto him are such as for the most part he effects by his word in and upon the hearts and consciences of men. Hence the difference that is between the various interpretations mentioned in the issue concurs in the same things, though the subject primarily spoken of be variously apprehended. Now that this is the word of God&#x27;s will, his enunciative word, his word written, spoken, preached, is by very many contended and pleaded on the ensuing reasons:—</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">1.</b> From the subject; &#x27;Because the Son of God, or Christ, is nowhere in the Scripture called ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, &quot;the Word,&quot; or &quot;Word of God,&quot; but only in the writings of John the apostle, as in his Gospel and the Revelation. By Paul he is everywhere, and in an especial manner in this epistle, called the Son, the Son of God, Jesus Christ; and nowhere is he termed by him the Word, or the Word of God.&#x27; This argument is made use of by all that are of this mind; but that it is not available to evince the conclusion intended shall immediately be made manifest.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">2.</b> From its attributes. They say, &#x27;The things here spoken of, and attributed unto the word of God, as that it is &quot;powerful, sharper than a two-edged sword, piercing to the dividing asunder of the soul and spirit,&quot; are not personal properties, or such things as may properly be ascribed unto a person, as the eternal Word of God is, but rather belong unto things, or a thing, such as is the word preached.&#x27; Now this must be particularly examined in our exposition of the words; wherein it will be made to appear, that the things here ascribed unto the word of God, taken together in their order and series, with respect unto the end designed, are such as cannot firstly and properly belong to any thing but a person, or an intelligent subsistence, though not merely as a person, but as a person acting for a certain end and purpose, such as the Son of God is; and this will also be evinced in our exposition of the words.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">3.</b> From the context. It is objected by Estius, &#x27;That the mentioning or bringing in of Christ, the Son of God, in this place is abrupt, and such as hath no occasion given unto it; for the apostle in the precedent verses is professedly treating about the gospel, and the danger they were in that should neglect it, or fall away from the profession of it. Hence it naturally follows, that he should confirm his exhortation by acquainting them with the power and efficacy of that word which they did despise.&#x27; But neither is there any force in this consideration: for,—(1.) We shall see that there is a very just occasion to introduce here the mention of the Lord Christ, and that the series of the apostle&#x27;s discourse and arguing did require it.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(2.)</b> It is the way and manner of the apostle, in this epistle, to issue his arguings and exhortations in considerations of the person of Christ, and the respect of what he had insisted on thereunto. This we have already manifested in several instances.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(3.)</b> Thus, in particular, when he had treated of the word of the law and of the gospel, he closeth his discourse by minding them of the punishment that should and would befall them by whom they were neglected. Now punishing is the act of a person, and not of the word, chap. 2:1–3. And there is the same reason for the introduction of the person of Christ in this place.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(4.)</b> Estius himself doth, and all must confess, that it is either God or Christ that is intended, verse 13, &quot;with whom we have to do,&quot; and &quot;before whose eyes all things are opened and naked.&quot; And if the order of the discourse admit of the introduction of the person of Christ in verse 13, no reason can be assigned why it may not do so in verse 12. Yea, it will be found very difficult, if possible, to preserve any tolerable connection of speech, and so to separate those verses that what is spoken of in the one should not be the subject of the other also.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">4.</b> Cameron argues, from the connection of the words, to prove the preaching of the word, and not the person of Christ, to be intended. For saith he, &#x27;The conjunction, καί, noteth the reason of the thing spoken of before; but that which precedes is a dehortation from the contempt of the gospel. And the reason hereof the apostle gives in these verses, in that those who forsake the gospel which they have once embraced are wont to be vexed in their consciences, as those who have denied the known truth. And although they seem to be quiet for a season, yet it is stupidness, and not peace, that they are possessed with. Now this judgment is often ascribed unto the word of God.&#x27;</p>

<p class="owen-d1">Ans. These things are somewhat obscurely proposed. The meaning seems to be, that the apostle threatens the Hebrews with the judging and disquieting power of the word when it is by any rejected. But this is inconsistent with the true design of the words, which we before laid down. Having exhorted them to perseverance, and to take heed that they neglected not the promise of entering into the rest of God through unbelief, he presseth them further to care, diligence, sincerity, and constancy, in the performance of the duty that he had exhorted them unto. And this he doth from the consideration of the person of Christ, the author of the gospel; as his manner is in all his arguings, to bring all to that point and centre. And as to his present purpose, suitably unto his exhortation and the duty which he enjoined them, he insists upon his ability to discern and discover all the secret frames and actings of their spirits, with all the ways and means whereby a declension in them might be begun or carried on. I do judge, therefore, that it is the eternal Word of God, or the person of Christ, which is the subject here spoken of, and that upon the ensuing reasons:—</p>

<p class="owen-d0 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">First</b> ὁ Λόγος, and ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, &quot;the Word,&quot; and &quot;the Word of God,&quot; is the proper name of Christ in respect of his divine nature, as the eternal Son of God. So is he called expressly, John 1:1, 2; Rev. 19:13, Καλεῖται τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, &quot;His name is called&quot; (or, &quot;this is his name,&quot;) &quot;the Word of God.&quot; This, therefore, being the name of Christ, where all things that are spoken of it do agree unto him, and there be no cogent reasons in the context to the contrary, he is presumed to be spoken of, nor will any rule of interpretation give countenance to the embracing of another sense.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">It is, as we heard before, excepted against this first reason, that Christ is called ὁ Λόγος, &quot;the Word,&quot; only in the writings of John the evangelist, and nowhere else in the New Testament, particularly not by our apostle in any of his epistles.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">Ans.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">1.</b> This observation can scarcely be made good; I am sure not convincingly. Luke the evangelist tells us that some were ἀπʼ ἀρχῆς αὐτόπται καὶ ὑπηρέται τοῦ Λόγου, chap. 1:2,—&quot;from the beginning eyewitnesses and ministers of the Word:&quot; that is, of the person of Christ; for these words are expounded, 1 John 1:1, &quot;That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled of the Word of life.&quot; They were αὐτόπται τοῦ Λόγοῦ,—&quot;eye-witnesses of the Word.&quot; How they could be said to be eye-witnesses of the word preached is not evident. Jerome renders the words, &quot;Sicut tradiderunt nobis, qui ab initio viderunt Sermonem et ministraverunt ei,&quot; Præfat. in Evangel.;—&quot;As they delivered unto us, who from the beginning themselves saw the Word, and ministered unto him.&quot; And ὑπηρέται must respect a person to whom those so called do minister, and not the word that is administered. In the same sense the word is used again most probably, Acts 20:32: Παρατίθεμαι ὑμᾶς, ἀδελφοὶ, τῷ Θεῷ, καὶ τῷ Λόγῳ τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ, τῷ δυναμένῳ ἐποικοδομῆσαι καὶ δοῦναι ὑμῖν κληρονομίαν·—&quot;I commend you, brethren, to God, and to the Word of his grace, which is able to build you up, and give you an inheritance.&quot; To be able to build us up, and give us an inheritance, is the property of a person; nor can they be ascribed to the word preached, without a forced prosopopœia, and such as is unusual in Scripture. Therefore this Λόγος τῆς χάριτος τοῦ Θεοῦ is the Son of God. And he is called &quot;the Word of his grace,&quot; either because he was given unto us of his mere grace, as he is elsewhere called &quot;the Son of his love;&quot; or τῆς χάριτος may be &quot;genitivus effecti,&quot; the Word that is the author and cause of grace; as God himself is called &quot;the God of peace and love,&quot; 2 Cor. 13:11. To him, therefore, are believers committed and commended by the apostle, as a recommendation is made of one man unto another in or by an epistle. See its sense in Acts 14:23; 1 Tim. 1:18; 1 Pet. 4:19. Now, the word of the gospel is said to be committed or commended unto us, 2 Tim. 2:2; so as we cannot, unless it be exceeding abusively, be said to be committed and commended thereunto. And if any will not admit the person of Christ to be here intended by &quot;the Word of God&#x27;s grace,&quot; I would supply an ellipsis, and read the text, &quot;I commend you to God, and the Word of his grace, even to him that is able;&quot; which I acknowledge the manner of the expression by the article τῷ δυναμένῳ will bear.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">2.</b> But whatever may be spoken concerning this phraseology in other places and in other epistles of this apostle, there is peculiar reason for the use of it here. I have observed often before, that in writing this epistle to the Hebrews, our apostle accommodates himself to the apprehensions and expressions that were then in use among the Hebrews, so far as they were agreeable unto the truth, rectifying them when under mistakes, and arguing with them from their own concessions and persuasions. Now at this time there was nothing more common or usual, among the Hebrews, than to denote the second subsistence in the Deity by the name of &quot;The Word of God&quot; They were now divided into two great parts; first, the inhabitants of Canaan, with the regions adjoining, and many old remnants in the east, who used the Syro-chaldean language, being but one dialect of the Hebrew; and, secondly, the dispersions under the Greek empire, who are commonly called Hellenists, who used the Greek tongue. And both these sorts at that time did usually, in their several languages, describe the second person in the Trinity by the name of &quot;The Word of God.&quot; For the former sort, or those who used the Syro-chaldean dialect, we have an eminent proof of it in the translation of the Scripture which, at least some part of it, was made about this time amongst them, commonly called the Chaldee Paraphrase; in the whole whereof the second person is mentioned under the name of דיי מימרא, &quot;Memra da- Iova,&quot; or the &quot;Word of God.&quot; Hereunto are all personal properties and all divine works assigned in that translation; which is an illustrious testimony to the faith of the old church concerning the distinct subsistence of a plurality of persons in the divine nature. And for the Hellenists, who wrote and expressed themselves in the Greek tongue, they used the name of ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, the &quot;Word of God,&quot; to the same purpose; as I have elsewhere manifested out of the writings of Philo, who lived about this time, between the death of our Saviour and the destruction of Jerusalem. And this one consideration is to me absolutely satisfactory as to the intention of the apostle in the using of this expression, especially seeing that all the things mentioned may far more properly and regularly be ascribed unto the person of the Son than unto the word as written or preached. And whosoever will take the pains to consider what occurs in the Targums concerning their דיי מימרא, the &quot;Word of God,&quot; and compare it with what the apostle here speaks, and the manner of its introduction, will, if I greatly mistake not, be of the same mind with myself. But I shall add yet some further considerations.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">3.</b> The introduction of ὁ Λόγος, or &quot;the Word,&quot; here, is with respect unto a commination or an admonition; for the design of it is to beget a reverence on fear in the minds of men about their deportment in the profession of the gospel, because of the consequents of disobedience in punishment and revenge. Now the Lord Christ is particularly termed the &quot;Word of God&quot; with respect unto the judgments that he exerciseth with regard unto his church and his gospel, Rev. 19:13. That administration, therefore, being here respected, gives occasion unto a peculiar ascription of that name unto him, the &quot;Word of God,&quot; who will destroy all the opposers and forsakers of the gospel.</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">4.</b> It cannot be denied, nor is it by any, but that it is the person of the Son, or of the Father, that is intended, verse 13. Indeed it is directly of the Son, as we shall manifest from the close of the words; but all confess God to be intended. Nor can these expressions, of &quot;all things manifest in his sight,&quot; and being &quot;opened and naked unto his eyes,&quot; be applied unto any other, or intend any other but God; and that it is the Son who is especially intended the close of the verse doth evince, πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ Λόγος. He speaks of &quot;him with whom we have to do.&quot; Some take πρὸς ὃν here for περὶ οὗ, &quot;concerning whom;&quot; ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος, &quot;nostra oratio est,&quot; &quot;our discourse is:&quot; which must needs denote the Son, concerning whom in this whole epistle he treats with the Hebrews. Ours, &quot;with whom we have to do;&quot; that is, in this matter,—who hath a concernment in us and our steadfastness or declension in profession. And this also properly and immediately designs the person of the Son. The precise sense of the words is, &quot;cui a nobis reddenda ratio est,&quot;—&quot;to whom we must give an account,&quot; both here and hereafter. So Chrysostom and the Syriac translation expressly. Principally this respects the last day&#x27;s account, called our λόγος, or &quot;ratiocinium:&quot; Heb. 13:17, &quot;They watch for your souls, ὡς λόγον ἀποδώσοντες,&quot;—&quot;as those that must give an account.&quot; Luke 16:2, Ἀπόδος τὸν λόγον,—&quot;Give an account of thy stewardship.&quot; Rom. 14:12, &quot;Every one of us λόγον δώσει,&quot;—&quot;shall give an account of himself unto God.&quot; 1 Pet. 4:5, Οἱ ἀποδώσουσι λόγον,—&quot;Who shall give an account unto him who is ready to judge the quick and the dead.&quot; And this account is certainly to be given up immediately to Jesus Christ, Acts 17:31, Rom. 14:9, 10. Nor is it any way obstructive to the embracing of this sense, that ὁ λόγος should be taken so diversely in the beginning of the 12th and end of the 13th verse, during the continuation of the same discourse. For such an antanaclasis is not only very frequent but very elegant: Ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, πρὸς ὃν ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος. See Matt. 8:22, 2 Cor. 5:21, John 1:11. It is therefore the person of Christ which is undeniably intended in the 13th verse, even he to whom we must give an account of our profession, of our faith and obedience. And the relative, αὐτοῦ, in the first clause of that verse, in &quot;his sight,&quot; can refer to nothing properly but ὁ Λόγος or &quot;Word of God,&quot; verse 12. And its dependence is clear thereon: &quot;Is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart; neither is there any creature that is not manifest in his sight.&quot; So a reason is assigned in the beginning of the 13th verse of what was affirmed in the close of the 12th: he is &quot;a discerner of the thoughts of the heart,&quot; because &quot;all things are manifest unto him.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d1 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">5.</b> The attributes here ascribed to the word, verse 12, do all of them properly belong unto the person of Christ, and cannot firstly and directly be ascribed to the gospel. This shall be manifested in the ensuing explication of the words:— (1.) It is said to be ζῶν, &quot;vivus,&quot; &quot;vivens,&quot;—&quot;living;&quot; which, as was observed, we have translated ambiguously, &quot;quick.&quot; Ζῶν is applied to God himself, as expressing a property of his nature, Matt. 16:16, 1 Tim. 4:10, Heb. 3:12. And it is also peculiarly ascribed unto Christ the mediator, Rev. 1:18. And he is ὁ ζῶν, &quot;the living one.&quot; And two things are intended in it:—[1.] That he who is so &quot;hath life in himself.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[2.]</b> That he is the &quot;Lord of life&quot; unto others. Both which are emphatically spoken of the Son.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[1.]</b> He &quot;hath life in himself,&quot; John 5:26; and, [2.] He is the &quot;Prince of life,&quot; Acts 3:15, or the author of it. He hath the disposal of the life of all, whereunto all our concernments temporal and eternal do belong. See John 1:4. And it is evident how suitable unto the purpose of the apostle the mention hereof at this time is. He minds the Hebrews that he with whom they have to do in this matter is &quot;the living one.&quot; As in like manner he had before exhorted them to &quot;take heed of departing from the living God,&quot; and afterwards warns them how &quot;fearful a thing it is to fall into the hands of the living God,&quot; chap. 10:31; so here, to dissuade them from the one and to awe them with the other, he minds them that &quot;the Word of God,&quot; with whom in an especial manner they have to do, is &quot;living.&quot; What is contained in this consideration hath been declared on chap. 3:13. Now this cannot properly be ascribed unto the word of the gospel. It is, indeed, the instrumental means of quickening the souls of men with spiritual life, or it is the instrument that the Lord Christ maketh use of to that purpose; but in itself it is not absolutely &quot;living,&quot;—it hath not life in itself, nor in its power. But Christ hath so; for &quot;in him is life, and the life is the light of men,&quot; John 1:4. And this one property of him with whom we have to do contains the two great motives unto obedience; namely, that on the one side he is able to support us in it, and reward us eternally for it; on the other, that he is able to avenge all disobedience. The one will not be unrewarded, nor the other unrevenged; for he is &quot;the living one&quot; with whom in these things we have to do.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(2.)</b> It is ἐνεργής, &quot;powerful.&quot; Power for operation is an act of life; and such as is the life of any thing, such is its power for operation. These things, life, power, and operation, answer one another. And this power signifies actual power, power acted or exerted,—actuated power, or power effectual in actual operation. Having therefore first assigned life to the Word of God, that is the principle of all power, life in himself, as being &quot;the living one,&quot; our apostle adds that he exerts that power of life in actual operation, when, where, and how he pleaseth. He is ἐνεργής. Ἐνεργέω, I confess, is a common word, signifying the efficacy of any thing in operation according to its principle of power; but it is that also whereby our apostle most frequently expresseth the almighty, effectual, operating power of God in and about spiritual things, 1 Cor. 12:6, 11, Gal. 2:8, 3:5, Eph. 1:11, Phil. 2:13, 1 Thess. 2:13, Eph. 1:19, Col. 2:12, and elsewhere. And this was necessary to be added to the property of life, to manifest that the Lord Christ, the Word of God, would effectually put forth his power in dealing with professors according to their deportment; which afterwards is expressed in sundry instances. And herein the apostle lets both the Hebrews and us know that the power that is in Christ lies not idle, is not useless, but is continually exercising itself towards us as the matter doth require. There is also, I acknowledge, an energy, an operative power in the word of God as written or preached; but it is not in it primarily, by virtue of a life or principle of power in itself, but only as a consequent of its being his word who is &quot;the living one,&quot; or &quot;as it is indeed the word of the living God.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d2">The original of the power of Christ in life, and the efficacy of it in operation, being laid down, he further declares it,—(1.) By its properties;</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(2.)</b> By its effects.</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Τομώτερος.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(1.)</b> The property of the Word, with respect unto the exercise of his power, is, that it is τομώτερος ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον. From τέμνω, to &quot;cut&quot; or &quot;divide,&quot; is τομός, &quot;scindens,&quot; &quot;incidens,&quot;—&quot;cleaving,&quot; &quot;cutting,&quot; or that which is &quot;vi incisoriâ præditus,&quot; endued with a cutting power; τομώτερος, in the comparative degree. Valla says he would render it &quot;incidentior,&quot; were that word used. So in Phocylides,—</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Ὅπλον τοι λόγος ἀνδρὶ τομώτερόν ἐστι σιδήρῳ.</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον. &quot;Telum ferro penetrantius;&quot; &quot;acutior,&quot; &quot;penetrantior&quot; (see the different translations of the word before); &quot;sharper,&quot; &quot;more piercing.&quot; Ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν μάχαιραν δίστομον. The preposition added to the comparative degree increaseth the signification; for it might have been said, τομὸς ὑπὲρ πᾶσαν, or τομώτερος πάσης μαχαίρας: but the construction used expresseth the greatest distance between the comparates,—&quot;than any two-edged sword.&quot; Δίστομος, that is, ἀμφίστομος,—&quot;gladius biceps, anceps, utrinque incidens;&quot; &quot;double-edged or mouthed, cutting every way.&quot; פִּי־חֶרֶב, &quot;the mouth of the sword,&quot; is a Hebraism, with such an elegance in the allusion as most languages have admitted it. The metaphor is doubtless taken from wild beasts, whom mankind first feared, that devour with their mouths; which when the sword began to be used for destruction, gave them occasion to call its edge by the name of its &quot;mouth:&quot; δίστομος, &quot;double mouthed,&quot; cutting each way, that leaves nothing unpierced whereunto it is applied. Christ in the exercise of his power is said to be &quot;more piercing than any two-edged sword;&quot; for so doth God oftentimes set forth himself and his power, with an allusion to things sensible, thereby to convey a notion and apprehension of them to our understandings. So he is said to be &quot;a consuming fire,&quot; and that he will be &quot;as a lion;&quot; things of great terror to men. This of a &quot;sword&quot; is often mentioned with respect unto the Lord Christ, Isa. 49:2; Rev. 1:16, &quot;Out of his mouth went a sharp two-edged sword.&quot; And it is principally assigned unto him with respect unto the exercise of his power in and by his word, which is called &quot;the sword of the Spirit,&quot; Eph. 6:17; the &quot;sword that is on his thigh,&quot; Ps. 45:3, which he hath in readiness when he goeth forth to subdue the souls of men to himself; as it is also &quot;the rod of his power,&quot; Ps. 110:2. But it is Christ himself who makes the word powerful and sharp: the principal efficiency is in himself, acting in and with it. That then which is here intended, is the spiritual, almighty, penetrating efficacy of the Lord Christ, in his dealing with the souls and consciences of men by his word and Spirit. And whereas there is a twofold use of a sword; the one natural, to cut or pierce through all opposition, all armour of defence; the other moral, to execute judgments and punishments, whence the sword is taken for the right and authority of punishing, and ofttimes for punishment itself, Rom. 13:4; here is an allusion unto it in both senses. The Lord Christ, by his word and Spirit, pierceth into the souls of men (as we shall see in the next clause), and that notwithstanding all the defence of pride, security, obstinacy, and unbelief, which they wrap up themselves in, according to the natural use of the sword. Again, he by them executes judgments on wicked men, hypocrites, false professors, and apostates. He &quot;smites the earth with the rod of his mouth, and slays the wicked with the breath of his lip,&quot; Isa. 11:4. He cuts off the life of their carnal hopes, false peace, worldly security, whatever they live upon, by the &quot;two-edged sword&quot; that proceeds out of his mouth. And the minding of the Hebrews hereof was exceedingly suited to his present purpose, as hath been declared. And in the pursuit of this double allusion are the ensuing expressions accommodated to the matter intended.</p>

<p class="owen-d2 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">(2.)</b> This power of the Word is described by its effects: Διϊκνούμενος ἄχρι μερισμοῦ ψυχῆς τε καὶ πνεύματος, ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν· καὶ κριτικὸς ἐνθυμήσεων καὶ ἐννοιῶν καρδίας. The act itself intended is in the first word, διϊκνούμενος. The object of that act is doubly expressed,—[1.] By &quot;soul and spirit;&quot; [2.]&quot;Joints and marrow;&quot; and [3.] There is the extent of this act with reference unto that object, expressing the effect itself, ἄχρι μερισμοῦ,—&quot;to the dividing of them.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Διϊκνούμενος. Διϊκνούμενος, &quot;perveniens,&quot; &quot;penetrans;&quot; &quot;piercing,&quot; say we, in answer to the sharpness before expressed. The word in other authors is variously rendered by &quot;pervado,&quot; &quot;permeo,&quot; &quot;pervenio,&quot; &quot;attingo,&quot;—&quot;to pass through,&quot; &quot;to reach unto,&quot; &quot;to attain an end;&quot; from ἵκω, &quot;to come.&quot; It is here, in the pursuit of the former allusion, used elegantly to express the power of Christ, as a sword piercing into the soul. And the meaning of the following expressions is, that it doth so into the innermost recesses, and as it were the secret chambers of the mind and heart. And this word is nowhere else used in the Scripture.</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Ψυχῆς τε καὶ πνεύματος. The object of this piercing is the &quot;soul and spirit.&quot; Some think that by ψυχή, the natural and unregenerate part of the soul is intended; and by πνεῦμα, that which is in it renewed and regenerate. And there is some ground for that explication of this distinction; for hence is a man wholly unregenerate called ψυχικός, 1 Cor. 2:14; say we, &quot;the natural man.&quot; And though ψυχή, absolutely used, doth denote either the being of the rational &quot;soul,&quot; or &quot;life,&quot; which is an effect thereof; yet as it is opposed to the &quot;spirit,&quot; or distinguished from it, it may denote the unregenerate part, as σάρξ, the &quot;flesh,&quot; doth, though absolutely it signifies one part of the material substance of the body. From hence is an unregenerate person denominated ἄνθρωπος ψυχικός. So the spiritual part is frequently called πνεῦμα, the &quot;spirit,&quot; as John 3:6; and a regenerate person πνευματικός, the &quot;spiritual man,&quot; 1 Cor. 2:15. According to this interpretation, the sense of the words is, that the Word of God, the Lord Christ, by his word and Spirit pierceth into the state of the soul, to discover who or what is regenerate amongst us or in us, and who or what is not so. The principles of these things are variously involved in the souls of men, so that they are not ofttimes discernible unto them in whom they are, as to whether of them is predominant. But the Lord Christ makes a μερισμός, a &quot;division&quot; with a distribution, referring all things in the soul to their proper source and original. Others judge, that whereas our apostle makes a distinction between soul and spirit, as he doth in other places, he intends by ψυχή, &quot;the soul,&quot; the affections, the appetites, and desires; and by πνεῦμα, &quot;the spirit,&quot; the mind or understanding, the τὸ ἡγεμονικόν, the &quot;conducting part&quot; of the soul. And it is most probable that he here intends the same: for setting out the penetrating power of the Word of God with reference unto the souls of men, he distributes the soul into as it were its principal constituent parts, or faculties of it; that is, the mind, that leads, conducts, and guides it; and the passions, that steer and balance it, wherein all the most secret recesses and springs of all its actings do lie. And this sense is confirmed from the following words, wherein the same thing is asserted under a different notion,—namely, of the &quot;joints and marrow.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d2">Ἁρμῶν τε καὶ μυελῶν. That which in the soul answers the joints and marrow in the body, by way of allusion, is that which is intended. Joints and marrow in themselves are things sensual and fleshly, that have no concern in this matter; but in the body they are doubly considerable,—</p>

<p class="owen-d3 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[1.]</b> Upon the account of their use; and so they are the ligaments of the whole, the principal and only means of communication to the members from the head, and among themselves. So this use of them is translated to spiritual things, Eph. 4:16. And by a luxation or discontinuation of them the whole body will be dissolved.</p>

<p class="owen-d3 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[2.]</b> On the account of their hiddenness and secrecy. They are undiscernible unto the eye of man, and it must be a sharp instrument or sword that pierceth unto them so as to divide them one from the other, whereby natural life will be destroyed. As these things are in the body for use and hiddenness, with respect unto their being pierced with a sword, so would the apostle have us to understand what he speaks of in reference unto the soul, the most useful and secret parts whereof are pierced and divided by the power of Christ; whence, if it be in a way of punishment, spiritual death doth ensue. And this is yet further confirmed in the last description which the apostle gives us of the Word of God from his actings and effects,—he is &quot;a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart;&quot; which yet he more clearly explains in the next verse, as we shall see in the opening of it. That, then, which in all these expressions is intended, is the absolute power and ability of the Son of God to judge of the rectitude and crookedness of the ways and walkings of the sons of men under their profession, from the inward frames of their minds and hearts unto all their outward duties and performances, either in perseverance or backsliding.</p>

<p class="owen-d3">Κριτικός. The last expression, κριτικὸς ἐνθυμήσεων καὶ ἐννοιῶν καρδίας, —&quot;is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart,&quot;—is plainly declarative of what is elsewhere ascribed unto him, namely, that he is καρδιογνώστης,—he that &quot;knoweth and searcheth the hearts of men.&quot; This is a peculiar property of God, and is often affirmed so to be, Jer. 17:10; 1 Sam. 16:7; Ps. 7:9; and this in an especial manner is ascribed to the Lord Christ, John 2:24, 25, 21:17; Rev. 2:23. This is eminently expressed in that confession of Peter, &quot;Lord, thou knowest all things, thou knowest that I love thee;&quot;—&#x27;By virtue of thine omniscience, whereby thou knowest all things, thou knowest my heart, and the love which I have therein unto thee.&#x27; Κριτικός, &quot;judex,&quot; &quot;discretor;&quot; one that, upon accurate inspection and consideration, judgeth and giveth sentence concerning persons and things. It differs from κριτής, a &quot;judge,&quot; as adding the act of judging unto the right and power of judgment. And this word alone, as it is here used, is sufficient to evince that the person of Christ is here principally intended, seeing it cannot be accommodated to the word as written or preached, in any tolerable manner.</p>

<p class="owen-d3">Καρδίας. Καρδίας. By the &quot;heart,&quot; as I have showed before, the whole soul and all the faculties of it, as constituting one rational principle of moral actions, is intended, and so includes the &quot;soul and spirit&quot; before mentioned. Here two things are ascribed unto it:—</p>

<p class="owen-d3">Ἐνθυμήσεων.</p>

<p class="owen-d3 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[1.]</b> Ἐνθυμήσεις, &quot;thoughts,&quot; &quot;cogitations,&quot; whatever is inwardly conceived, ἐν τῷ θυμῷ, &quot;in the mind;&quot; with a peculiar respect unto the irascible appetite called לֵכ מַחְשְׁבֹת יֵצֶר, Gen. 6:5, &quot;the figment of the cogitations of the heart,&quot;—the thoughts which are suggested by the inclinations of the affections, with their commotions and stirrings in the heart or mind.</p>

<p class="owen-d3">Ἔννοιῶν.</p>

<p class="owen-d3 owen-mk"><b class="owen-num">[2.]</b> Ἔννοιαι, &quot;designs&quot; or &quot;purposes,&quot; inwardly framed ἐν τῷ νόῳ, &quot;in the understanding.&quot; Sometimes this word signifies the moral principles of the mind, by which it is guided in its actings. Hence are the κοιναὶ ἔννοιαι, or &quot;common principles&quot; that men are directed by in what they do. And here it denotes the principles that men are guided by in their actings, according to which they frame their actual purposes and intentions. Upon the whole matter, the design of the apostle in these words is to declare the intimate and absolute acquaintance that the Word of God hath with the inmost frames, purposes, desires, resolutions, and actings of the minds of professors; and the sure, unerring judgment which he makes of them thereby.</p>
