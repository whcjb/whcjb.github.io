# 希伯来书 2:7

<div class="owen-vg-sub">约翰·欧文注释 · Ver. 7</div>

<div class="owen-scr" markdown="0"><p class="owen-scr-en">[2.] The especial instances wherein this visitation of God expressed itself are contained in verse 7, and therein referred unto two heads: 1st. Man's depression and humiliation; 2dly. His exaltation and glory.</p></div>

<p class="owen-d0">1st. The first is expressed in these words, &quot;Thou hast made him lower for a little while than the angels.&quot; This was a part of God&#x27;s visitation; and though not that which was immediately intended by the apostle, yet that whereof he intends to make great use in his progress. That these words intend not the exaltation of the nature of mere man, as if they should intimate, that such is his dignity he is made but a little less than angels, and how destructive that sense is unto the apostle&#x27;s intention and application of the words, we shall afterwards declare. Three things are here expressed:—(1st.) The act of God, in making of him low, or lessening of him; (2dly.) The measure of that depression, &quot;than the angels;&quot; (3dly.) His duration in that state and condition, &quot;a little while.&quot;</p>

<p class="owen-d0">(1st.) חָסֵד, the word used by the psalmist is rendered by the apostle ἐλαττόω, and that properly. They both signify a diminution of state and condition, a depression of any one from what he before enjoyed. And this in the first place belongs unto God&#x27;s visitation. And the acting of the will of Christ in this matter, suitably unto the will of the Father, is expressed by words of the same importance: Ἐκένωσεν ἑαυτόν, &quot;He emptied himself;&quot; and Ἐταπείνωσεν ἑαυτόν, &quot;He humbled himself,&quot; Phil. 2:7, 8: denoting a voluntary depression from the glory of a former state and condition. In this humiliation of Christ in our nature, how much of that care and ἐπισκοπής, inspection and visitation of God, was contained, is known.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">(2dly.) The measure of this humiliation and depression is expressed in reference unto angels, with whom he is now compared by the apostle,—he was made less than the angels. This the Hebrews had seen and knew, and might from his humiliation raise an objection against what the apostle asserted about his preference above them. Wherefore he acknowledged that he was made less than they, shows that it was foretold that so he should be, and in his following discourse gives the reasons why it was so to be. And he speaks not of the humiliation of Christ absolutely, which was far greater than here it is expressed by him, as he afterwards declares, but only with respect unto angels, with whom he compares him; and it is therefore sufficient to his purpose at present to show that he was made lower than they: מֵאֱלֹהִים, παρʼ ἀγγέλους. Jerome renders the words in the psalm, &quot;à Deo,&quot; &quot;than God;&quot; and Faber Stapulensis had a long contest with Erasmus to prove that they should be so rendered in this place; which is plainly to contradict the apostle, and to accuse him of corrupting the word of God. Besides, the sense contended for by him and others is absurd and foolish, namely, that the human nature of Christ was made little less than God, and humbled that it might be so, when it was infinitely less than the divine nature, as being created. The LXX. and all old Greek translations read &quot;angels.&quot; That elohim is often used to denote them we have proved before. The Targum hath מלאכיא, &quot;angels;&quot; and the scope of the place necessarily requires that sense of the word. God, then, in his visitation of the nature of man in the person of his Son, put it, and therein him that was invested with it, into a condition of wants and straits, and humbled him beneath the condition of angels, for the blessed ends afterwards declared. For although, from his incarnation and birth, the angels adored his person as their Lord, yet in the outward condition of his human nature he was made exceedingly beneath that state of glory and excellency which the angels are in a constant enjoyment of.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">(3dly.) There is a space of time, a duration, intended for this condition. He made him lower, מְעַט, βραχύ τι, &quot;for a little while,&quot; or, &quot;a short season.&quot; That מְעַט is often used in that sense, and that that is the proper notation of βραχύ τι, we have showed before. But that which renders that sense of the words here unquestionable, is the apostle&#x27;s precise restraining them thereunto in verse 9, as we shall see. It was but for a little while that the person of Christ in the nature of man was brought into a condition more indigent than the state of angels is exposed unto; neither was he for that season made a little, but very much lower than the angels. And had this been the whole of his state, it could not have been an effect of that inexpressible love and care which the psalmist so admires; but seeing it is but for a little continuance, and that for the blessed ends which the apostle declares, nothing can more commend them unto us.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">2dly. There is another effect of God&#x27;s visitation of man, in his exaltation; expressed, (1st.) In the dignity whereunto he advanced him; and, (2dly.) In the rule and dominion that he gave unto him.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">(1st.) For the first, he &quot;crowned him with glory and honour.&quot; עֲטָרָה is &quot;insigne regium,&quot; the badge and token of supreme and kingly power. Hence when David complains of the straitening and diminution of his power or rule, he says, his &quot;crown was profaned unto the ground,&quot; Ps. 89:39; that is, made contemptible and trampled on. To be crowned, then, is to be invested with sovereign power, or with right and title thereunto; as it was with Solomon, who was crowned during the life of his father. Nor is it an ordinary crown that is intended, but one accompanied with &quot;glory and honour.&quot; To be crowned with glory and honour, is to have a glorious and honourable crown, or rule and sovereignty: וְהָדָר כָבוֹד. The first denotes the weight of this crown; כָבוֹד, &quot;weight of glory,&quot; from כָבַד, &quot;to be heavy;&quot; βάρος δόξης, &quot;a weight of glory,&quot; as the apostle speaks in allusion to the primitive signification of this word, 2 Cor. 4:17: the other, its beauty and glory: both, authority and majesty. How Christ was thus crowned, we have at large showed on the first chapter.</p>

<p class="owen-d0">(2dly.) This sovereignty is attended with actual rule; wherein, [1st.] The dominion itself is expressed; and, [2dly.] The extent of it. [1st.] &quot;Thou madest him to have dominion over the works of thy hands.&quot; תַּמְשִׁילֵהוּ, &quot;madest him to rule;&quot; κατέστησας αὐτὸν ἐπί, &quot;appointedst him in authority over.&quot; He had actual rule and dominion given him upon his coronation. And, [2dly.] The extent of this dominion is &quot;the works of God&#x27;s hands.&quot; And lest any, from this indefinite expression, should think this rule limited either to the things mentioned before by the psalmist, verse 3, called &quot;the work of God&#x27;s fingers,&quot;—that is, the heavens, the moon, and the stars; or in the following distribution of things here below, into sheep, oxen, fowls, and fish, verses 7, 8,—that is, all the creatures here below; he adds an amplification of it in a universal proposition, Πάντα ὑπέταξε, &quot;He hath put all things&quot; without exception &quot;in subjection unto him.&quot; And to manifest his absolute and unlimited power, with the unconditional subjection of all things unto him, he adds, that they are placed ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν αὐτοῦ‚ &quot;under his&quot; very &quot;feet;&quot;—an expression setting forth a dominion every way unlimited and absolute.</p>
