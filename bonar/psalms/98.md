<p><span class="bn-vr">1–9</span>RYLAND (in "Psalms restored to Messiah,") thinks that as the Jews held that Moses wrote Psalm 90 and onward to the 98, it may be to this Psalm that Rev. 15 alludes, as "The Song of Moses and the Lamb." This is improbable; but the Psalm suits that time. The kingdom and the King have arrived; the blessedness of that happy day has been celebrated. But the harp cannot be silent yet! Another song on the same key! Another sweet and solemn melody on the same theme; but with this special addition, the Lord's faithfulness to Israel,</p>

<p>Hengstenberg remarks that this Psalm is full of allusion to Isaiah. At any rate, this Psalm and Isaiah, whichever was the earlier, answer to one another, as seraph to seraph, celebrating "wonders," "salvation with his right hand," "his holy arm," "his righteousness revealed." And may not the clause in verse 2,</p>

<p>"He hath remembered his mercy and truth,"</p>

<p>be considered as equivalent to "full of grace and truth" in John 1:17? That grace and truth is now to be revealed to Israel in particular, for he who is the fountain of it is to dwell among them—his throne stretched over Jerusalem as a rainbow spans the plain beneath, and his sceptre swayed over earth to its utmost ends.</p>

<p>"Sing to Jehovah with the harp!</p>

<p>With the harp and voice of psaltery!</p>

<p>With cornets and sound of trumpet (as at the bringing up of the ark to Zion, and as in 1 Kings 1:34, when Solomon was crowned).</p>

<p>Raise the peal of melody</p>

<p>Before THE KING, Jehovah!"</p>

<p>And as at the commencement of a reign in Israel, we read of the shout, "Let the king live!" (2 Kings 11:12, and 9:13,) "Jehu is king!" and as they clapped the hand (2 Kings 11:12), as well as shouted and blew the trumpet (2 Kings 9:13), so we find all these recognitions of The King in this Psalm—"The rivers clap their hands," and "the hills shout for joy," for the king foretold in David's last words has at length come (2 Sam. 23:3), to rule over men in the fear of the Lord.</p>

<p>It is the only Psalm called simply <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מִזְמֹר</span> "Psalm," without addition. Some say that the reason is, there are so many verses that have some form of the root <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">זָמַר</span>, verses 1–8, &amp;c. Hengstenberg accounts for it by supposing it the lyrical accompaniment of the more directly prophetical preceding Psalm, and the lyrical echo of the second part of Isaiah. It is at least interesting to notice, that a song of Zion which so exults in the king's arrival should be called pre-eminently, <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מִזְמֹר</span>; as if the Psalm of Psalms were that which celebrates</p>

<p>Israel, and Earth at large, blessed in Messiah's Advent.</p>
