<p>IT may be helpful to a right understanding of the Psalms we now approach, and may increase our interest in them, to begin with a synoptical view of the fifteen which are clustered together, and go by the name of "Songs of Degrees." The progression of thought and subject thus becomes clear:—</p>

<p>The Pilgrim Train and its Leader—</p>

<p>Psa. 120.—Weary with the strife of tongues. (Comp. Psa. 81:6.)</p>

<p>Psa. 121.—Commit themselves to Jehovah alone, as they journey forth.</p>

<p>Psa. 122.—Sing of the City of Habitation, to which they journey.</p>

<p>Psa. 123.—Cast an upward look amid the contempt of those they meet with.</p>

<p>At a stage in their way which we might call Ebenezer—</p>

<p>Psa. 124.—Give praise for deliverance hitherto.</p>

<p>Psa. 125.—Express confidence of being kept faithful, through Jehovah's faithfulness, to the end.</p>

<p>Psa. 126.—Sing of the joys Jehovah has given, and will give, to his servants.</p>

<p>Psa. 127.—Cease from carefulness, and ascribe the success to Jehovah.</p>

<p>Psa. 128.—Pronounce blessing on all the fearers of God.</p>

<p>Psa. 129.—Review their past sufferings in hope.</p>

<p>Psa. 130.—Relate their earnest cry in trouble, and the rich result.</p>

<p>Psa. 131.—Express their contentment with Jehovah's will.</p>

<p>Psa. 132.—Remind Jehovah of pledges of favour to Zion, and are answered.</p>

<p>Psa. 133.—Admire and sing of the unity of those met in the Holy City, their habitation.</p>

<p>Psa. 134.—Call for unceasing praise from all Jehovah's servants.</p>

<p>We adopt the idea of these fifteen Psalms being in some sense the songs of those who went up to Jerusalem to worship. They do not give us the inward experience of individuals only; they bear reference to Israel at large; for even when, as in the case of the 130th and 131st, the strain has a personal aspect, the closing verse sings of Israel.<sup class="bn-fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup></p>

<p>In the singular, <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מַעֲלָה</span> frequently designates the going up to a higher spot, e. g., the ascent of Bethhoron, the ascent of Luhith, and in Ezra 7:9, the going up from Babylon. In the plural it is used for the steps of Solomon's throne, and in Ezekiel (40:26, 31, 34), for the steps of the temple-gates. The use of <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מַעֲלוֹת</span> for the degrees or steps of a dial, has been fully illustrated by recent discoveries in Assyria, which prove that the sun-dial was a series of steps, or terraces, on which a pole cast its shadow. It would appear, therefore, that the name "Song of the Steps" is a poetical one, designating Psalms which specially suited the circumstances of those who go up to the Temple.</p>

<p>Hengstenberg remarks that they are grouped round the 127th, which is Solomon's; and we may add that that central "Song of Degrees," or steps, has special reference to "The House," or Temple.</p>

<div class="bn-notes">
<p id="fn-1"><a class="bn-back" href="#fnref-1">1</a>This feature of these fifteen Psalms is itself sufficient to set aside the idea of a writer in the <em>Jewish Chronicle</em>, that they were specially for <em>domestic use</em>, and get their name from the steps, or ascent to the <em>house-top</em>, where devout Jews were wont to worship. Some have conjectured that the title "Degrees" may refer to the musical instruments used in chaunting them. The common idea that the name refers to the steps of the Temple, is that expressed in Parker's old translation—"These fifteen Psalms next following be songs benamed of steps and stairs, for that the choir on them did sing."</p>
</div>
