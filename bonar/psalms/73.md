<p><span class="bn-vr">1–28</span>A SORT of historical series begins here, relating to Israel's position in the world (73), to their temple (74), their land (75), God's deeds therein (76, 77), God's dealings in days past (78), Israel's desolation (79), and prayer regarding the same (80).</p>

<p>"A Psalm of Asaph;" perhaps one of those specially sung by Hezekiah's appointment at the altar (2 Chron. 29:30): and what more fit to be sung there when the ascending smoke and poured-out blood declared in type Jehovah's unspeakable gift, and so seemed to say, "Yes, God is good to Israel!" (2 Chron. 5:13)</p>

<p>How well it follows the last Psalm! As if Asaph had been singing it, and thereupon had felt all his surmises and faithless fears dissipated by the triumphant prospects held out there to the people of Messiah. It is a Psalm, not about Messiah himself, but about "his people" (ver. 10), about "Israel" (ver. 1); about the members, not the Head. It is uttered in the presence of the Head; but it tells how its members have often been nearly "offended in Him" (Luke 7:23). Had Asaph lived in Herod's day, such suspicious surmises as are expressed in verses 3–9 might have been raised in his soul, by seeing the Baptist first in the dungeon, and then in the tomb, while Herod ruled and rioted in luxury. Horsley remarks on the first word <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">אַךְ</span>, "It expresses the state of mind of a person meditating a difficult question, in which he is much interested and can hardly come to a conclusion."</p>

<p>Verse 4 should be rendered—"There are no death-bands to them" (Horsley); "they are never fettered with death" (Hengst), i. e., there are no death-bringing circumstances in their lot. They escape the annoyances and reproaches which God's people meet with (see 1 Cor. 10:13); their "pride" is their "ornamental chain" (ver. 6), and (ver. 7) "the imagination of their hearts overflows"—that is, speaks out, or vents itself;</p>

<p>"They speak of oppression from on high" (ver. 9) (Hengst);</p>

<p>as if they were out of reach of danger, aloft on their rock; or as old Sandys renders the line, "They speak like thunder from the troubled sky." Hence,</p>

<p>"His people return hither" (ver. 10);</p>

<p>i. e., God's people return to the state of mind described in verse 2, or to this sight which causes the unbelieving surmises. To quote Sandys again, as giving the right sense—</p>

<p>"The good not seldom, through their scandal, stray."</p>

<p>How like a desponding man's words is verse 12, "Yet they prosper for ever," or more literally, "They are everlasting prosperers!" But now, the likelihood of giving occasion to others to stumble crosses his mind; and forthwith the same Spirit who suggested that consideration, leads Asaph in his thoughts (as some understand the words of verse 17) to the sanctuary. Standing there, the very thought of the Holy One on his Throne is enough to remind him of what must be the end of these ungodly ones; but more especially is the remembrance that there is a resurrection day—a day when God will arise and scatter these dreams of earthly felicity (ver. 20).</p>

<p>"O Lord, when thou awakest, (see Psa. 17:15) thou wilt despise their splendid show,</p>

<p>As one does a dream, when he awakes out of it!"</p>

<p>Telling his grief and shame because of such unbelief, confessing himself a beast<sup class="bn-fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup> or brute, he yet returns to sing that, notwithstanding all this, God has not forsaken him, and never will—</p>

<p>"And I continually am with thee!</p>

<p>And thou holdest me fast by my right hand."<sup class="bn-fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2">2</a></sup> (Ver. 23.)</p>

<p>I am in the wilderness, and thou art my guide, and wilt "receive me," as thou didst Enoch (Gen. 5:22, same word). The Hebrew words are rather obscure, but this may be because of the ideas rushing through the mind of the Psalmist, so various and so fast. They are literally rendered thus—</p>

<p>"Thou wilt lead me by thy counsel.</p>

<p>And afterwards, glory! Thou wilt receive me!"</p>

<p>Not unlike Psa. 49:6—"God shall redeem my soul from the grave" (equivalent to "afterwards glory!") "for he shall receive me."</p>

<p>Thus God is "the rock of my heart;" my heart rests on him as on a solid basis. All foes, and all prosperous wicked men, are from this point seen as ruined. No wonder. For has glory come? has the glory of the kingdom dawned on us? has the Lord himself welcomed us in? has he given us a place beside himself? Then, from this height we look down and see the impotency and ruin of Antichrist and all such opposers of God, "who go a-whoring from thee." (Comp. Rev. 17:5, "mother of harlots.") Meanwhile we draw near to God, reenter paradise, enjoy our lost fellowship; and our great employment is to praise Him, all clouds of providence being now cleared away, and no more unbelief to hinder our "telling of all his works."</p>

<p>The tone of this Psalm, especially of the latter part, is that of James 5:7, 8—"Be patient, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord." The prevailing topic may be said to be,</p>

<p class="bn-sum">"Messiah's people almost offended in Him."</p>
