<p><span class="bn-vr">1–13</span>HE has brought them to their desired haven!</p>

<p>"O God! my heart is fixed—</p>

<p>My heart is fixed."</p>

<p>No more tossed, no more wandering.</p>

<p>"I will sing and give praise;</p>

<p>Awake, my glory, awake!"</p>

<p>A call to celebrate a greater victory than Deborah's (Judges 5:12) —"Awake, my glory"—i. e., my tongue, the best member I have for the purpose; or, my heart (Hengstenberg), fitted to be a harp that may sound thy name.</p>

<p>"Awake, psaltery and harp!</p>

<p>I will awake the morning!"</p>

<p>For his mercy is great—that mercy sung of lately (Psa. 107:1, and ver. 43). It is "from above the heavens" (<span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מעל שמים</span>); i. e., coming down on us, as do drops of a fertilizing shower. Even as the "Peace on earth," of Luke 2:14, was first "Peace in heaven" (Luke 19:38).</p>

<p>We have already had the words of this Psalm in two others—viz., the 57 and 60. But here the joyful and the triumphant portions of these two are joined in one, to form a lofty melody ("A song, a psalm," like Psa. 30 in the title), celebrating Israel's return and Messiah's triumph.<sup class="bn-fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup> Messiah, and all Israel along with him, and every saint "rejoicing with Jerusalem" (Isa. 65:18), utter this Psalm. Messiah, as Leader, speaks, in verse 9, in his own name (an intentional variation from Psalm 60:8)—</p>

<p>"Moab I will use as a vessel in which to wash my feet (when the journey is over),</p>

<p>Over Edom I will cast my shoe (as one does to his servant),</p>

<p>Over Philistia I will raise the shout of joy."</p>

<p>Both in this Psalm and Psalm 60, the words in verse 10—"Who shall lead me?"—are in the present participle, <span class="bn-he" lang="he" dir="rtl">מִי יוֹבֵל</span>—"Who is my leader?"—thus admitting of application to the past, while they may be prospective also; like the expression in Heb. 13:7, "Remember Τῶν ἡγουμένων ὑμῶν," your rules. The speaker asks—</p>

<p>"Who is he that leadeth me to the strong city?</p>

<p>Who hath led me into Edom?</p>

<p>Is it not thou, O God, who hadst rejected us,</p>

<p>And didst not go forth with our armies?"</p>

<p>Then, going forward in the Lord's name, and renouncing man's strength, He and His enter on possession, saying—</p>

<p>"Through God we shall do valiantly;</p>

<p>He it is that treads down our enemies."</p>

<p>"It is he that bruises Satan under our feet." may every saint reply; and, when Israel's day has come, every saint shall find himself blessed in their blessing. And so shall the Church join</p>

<p class="bn-sum">Messiah, in behalf of restored Israel, raising the shout of victory.</p>

<div class="bn-notes">
<p id="fn-1"><a class="bn-back" href="#fnref-1">1</a>Ryland says, that in Psalm lvii. and lx. the words were prophetic and consolatory; here they are rather eucharistic, and descriptive of what God has prepared for his own.</p>
</div>
