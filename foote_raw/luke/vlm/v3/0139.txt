From their being at liberty, however, to take interest from strangers, or those of other nations, when yet they were commanded "not to vex or oppress them,"[FN:*] as well as from the way in which it is spoken of in this parable, it is certain that there is nothing sinful in the custom of taking interest, provided it be not practised in an oppressive way towards the poor, or to an avaricious and exorbitant degree towards any.—Though there were no regular and splendid institutions like our banks in ancient times, the original word,[FN:†] which strictly signifies a "table," is well rendered "bank" in this place, because we have nothing else which comes so near the idea to be expressed. We read elsewhere of the tables of the "money-changers."[FN:‡] These men dealt in exchanging different kinds of coin, and in borrowing and lending money.

As a farther proof of his displeasure with the slothful servant, the master "*said unto them that stood by, Take from him the pound, and give it to him that hath ten pounds.*" This seems to imply that the master generously gave the diligent servant what he had gained to himself.
